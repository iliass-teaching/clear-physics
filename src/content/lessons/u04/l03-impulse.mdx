<Step kind="learn" title="Impulse" minutes={10}>

A force that acts for a time changes the momentum of an object.

The **impulse** is the force × the time for which the force acts. It is equal to the **change in momentum**:

$$
\text{impulse} = F\Delta t = \Delta(mv)
$$

The unit of impulse is the **newton second (N s)**. It is the same as kg m/s.

<WorkedExample
  question="A tennis racket hits a ball with an average force of 200 N for 0.005 s. Calculate the impulse on the ball."
  given={['$F = 200\\,\\text{N}$', '$\\Delta t = 0.005\\,\\text{s}$']}
  find="the impulse"
  answer="$1\,\text{N s}$"
>

$\text{impulse} = F\Delta t = 200 \times 0.005$

$\text{impulse} = 1\,\text{N s}$ (so the momentum of the ball changes by $1\,\text{kg m/s}$)

</WorkedExample>

**Be careful with bouncing:** a ball that hits a wall at $+5\,\text{m/s}$ and bounces back at $-3\,\text{m/s}$ has a change in velocity of $-3 - 5 = -8\,\text{m/s}$, not $2\,\text{m/s}$.

</Step>

<Step kind="learn" title="Force = rate of change of momentum" minutes={10}>

Divide both sides of $F\Delta t = \Delta p$ by $\Delta t$:

$$
F = \frac{\Delta p}{\Delta t}
$$

The **resultant force** is the **change in momentum per unit time**.

So, for the **same change in momentum**:

- a **short** time gives a **big** force;
- a **long** time gives a **small** force.

### Safety in a crash

In a crash, a car and its passengers lose all their momentum. Safety features make this change take **longer**, so the force is **smaller**:

- **crumple zones**: the front of the car squashes slowly;
- **airbags**: the head slows down over a longer time;
- **seat belts**: they stretch a little;
- **helmets**: the soft foam inside squashes.

This is also why you move your hands back when you catch an egg.

<WorkedExample
  question="A 70 kg passenger moving at 15 m/s stops in a crash. Calculate the force on him if he stops in 0.05 s (hitting the dashboard), and in 0.5 s (with an airbag)."
  given={['$m = 70\\,\\text{kg}$', '$\\Delta v = 15\\,\\text{m/s}$', '$\\Delta t = 0.05\\,\\text{s}$ or $0.5\\,\\text{s}$']}
  find="the force in each case"
  answer="$21\,000\,\text{N}$ without the airbag, $2100\,\text{N}$ with it"
>

$\Delta p = 70 \times 15 = 1050\,\text{kg m/s}$

without airbag: $F = \dfrac{1050}{0.05} = 21\,000\,\text{N}$

with airbag: $F = \dfrac{1050}{0.5} = 2100\,\text{N}$ (ten times smaller)

</WorkedExample>

</Step>

<Step kind="do" title="Egg drop challenge" minutes={12}>

Design a package to protect a raw egg dropped from 2 m.

**Rules:** you can use paper, straws, tape and cotton wool. No more than 30 cm in any direction.

1. Draw your design and label it.
2. Explain, using **impulse** and **time**, how it reduces the force on the egg.
3. Test it (outside, or over a tray).

*Start your explanation like this: "The egg loses the same momentum, but the … makes the time longer, so …"*

<Step kind="teacher" title="Teacher notes">

**Extended content** (1.6 S2, S4).

**Timing:** starter 5 min, learn 20 min, egg drop 12 min, practice 18 min, exit ticket 5 min.

**Common mistakes:**

- In a bounce, subtracting the speeds instead of using signed velocities.
- Thinking safety features reduce the change in momentum (they increase the time).
- Mixing up impulse (N s) and force (N).

**Egg drop:** the full challenge can take longer. Do the design and explanation in class, and the test in another lesson if needed.

**Language support:**

- Read $\Delta$ as "change in". Sentence frame: *"For the same change in momentum, a longer time gives a … force."*

**Differentiation:**

- Support: start with Level 1 (impulse = force × time).
- Stretch: a ball hits a wall and bounces back with the same speed. Is the impulse zero? (No: the change in velocity is $2v$.)

**Safety:** clean up broken eggs quickly. Check for egg allergies.

</Step>
