<Step kind="starter" title="Ørsted's compass" minutes={5}>

In 1820, Hans Christian Ørsted noticed that a compass needle moved when he switched on a current in a nearby wire. What does this tell you about an electric current?

</Step>

<Step kind="learn" title="A straight wire" minutes={14}>

A current in a wire makes a **magnetic field** around it.

<Figure svg={wireFieldSvg()} caption="The field around a straight wire, seen from above. ⊙: current out of the page; ⊗: current into the page." width="34rem" />

- The field lines are **circles** around the wire.
- **Direction: the right-hand grip rule.** Hold the wire in your right hand, with your thumb pointing in the direction of the (conventional) current. Your fingers curl in the direction of the field.

**Extended:**

- The field is **strongest close to the wire**. Further away, it gets weaker: the circles are further apart.
- A **bigger current** gives a **stronger** field.
- **Reversing the current** reverses the direction of the field.

</Step>

<Step kind="learn" title="A solenoid" minutes={12}>

A **solenoid** is a long coil of wire.

<Figure svg={solenoidSvg()} caption="The field of a solenoid is like the field of a bar magnet." width="34rem" />

- Outside, the field is like the field of a **bar magnet**: one end is a N pole, the other end is a S pole.
- **Inside**, the field is **strong** and almost **uniform** (parallel, equally spaced lines).
- Reversing the current swaps the N and S poles.

**Extended:** the field gets **stronger** with a bigger current and with more turns. It is strongest inside the solenoid.

</Step>

<Step kind="do" title="Find the patterns" minutes={16}>

**Straight wire:** push a thick wire vertically through the middle of a horizontal card. Connect it to a low-voltage supply (a few amps; switch on only for a few seconds).

1. Sprinkle iron filings on the card and tap it. What pattern do you see?
2. Put 4 plotting compasses around the wire. Which way do they point? Reverse the current. What changes?

**Solenoid:** wind a coil around a cardboard tube (or use a ready-made solenoid on a card).

3. Use iron filings and plotting compasses again. Which end is the N pole? Reverse the current and check.

</Step>

<Step kind="teacher" title="Teacher notes">

**Core and Extended:** patterns, directions and experiments (4.5.3 C1, C2) are Core. Strength variation and the effect of changing the current (S4, S5) are Extended.

**Timing:** starter 5 min, wire 14 min, solenoid 12 min, practical 16 min, check 8 min, exit ticket 5 min.

**Practical tips:** you need a big current (a few amps) for the straight-wire pattern; switch on only briefly. A solenoid with many turns shows a clear pattern with a smaller current.

**Common mistakes:**

- Using the left hand for the grip rule.
- Drawing field lines that start or end on the wire.
- Using the electron flow instead of the conventional current.

**Language support:**

- *grip* = hold tightly; *curl* = bend round.
- ⊙ (dot) = the current comes towards you, like the point of an arrow; ⊗ (cross) = it goes away, like the feathers of an arrow.

**Differentiation:**

- Support: a printed card with 4 compasses to complete.
- Stretch: how could you make the field of a solenoid much stronger without changing the current? (More turns; add a soft-iron core.)

**Safety:** the wire gets hot with a big current: switch on for a few seconds only.

</Step>
