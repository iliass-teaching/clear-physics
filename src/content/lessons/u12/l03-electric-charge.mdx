<Step kind="learn" title="Two kinds of charge" minutes={12}>

There are two kinds of **electric charge**: **positive (+)** and **negative (−)**.

<Figure svg={chargesSvg()} caption="Forces between charges." width="28rem" />

- Positive charges **repel** positive charges.
- Negative charges **repel** negative charges.
- Positive charges **attract** negative charges.

All matter is made of atoms. An atom has a positive **nucleus** and negative **electrons** around it. Normally an object has the same amount of + and − charge: it is **neutral** (uncharged).

**Extended:** charge is measured in **coulombs** (C).

</Step>

<Step kind="learn" title="Charging by friction" minutes={12}>

When we rub two insulators together, **electrons** (negative charges) move from one to the other.

<Figure svg={frictionSvg()} caption="A polythene rod rubbed with a cloth." width="30rem" />

- The object that **gains** electrons becomes **negative**.
- The object that **loses** electrons becomes **positive**.
- **Only negative charges (electrons) move.** The positive charges stay in the nuclei, which do not move.

Examples: a **polythene** rod rubbed with a cloth gains electrons: it becomes **negative**. An **acetate** rod rubbed with a cloth loses electrons: it becomes **positive**.

**Detecting charge:**

- A charged object attracts small pieces of paper.
- A **gold-leaf electroscope**: touch the metal cap with a charged object. The charge spreads to the rod and the gold leaf. They have the same charge, so they **repel**: the leaf rises.

<Figure svg={electroscopeSvg()} caption="A gold-leaf electroscope." width="30rem" />

</Step>

<Step kind="do" title="Charge it" minutes={14}>

**Equipment:** a polythene rod, an acetate rod, a dry cloth, a watch glass (or a stand with a stirrup to hang a rod), small pieces of paper, an electroscope (if available).

1. Rub the polythene rod with the cloth. Bring it near the pieces of paper. What happens?
2. Rub the polythene rod again and put it on the watch glass, so it can turn freely.
3. Rub a second polythene rod. Bring it near the end of the first one. What happens?
4. Now rub the acetate rod and bring it near the polythene rod. What happens?
5. Explain your results with the words *gains*, *loses*, *electrons*, *attract* and *repel*.

</Step>

<Step kind="teacher" title="Teacher notes">

**Core and Extended:** 4.2.1 C1–C4 are Core; the coulomb (S7) is Extended.

**Timing:** starter 5 min, learn 24 min, practical 14 min, check 12 min, exit ticket 5 min.

**Tips:** electrostatic experiments work best on dry days. Warm the rods and the cloth a little to dry them.

**Common mistakes:**

- "Positive charges move to the rod."
- "Rubbing creates charge." Charge is not created; electrons are transferred. The total charge stays the same.
- Thinking a charged object only attracts objects with the opposite charge (it also attracts neutral objects, like small pieces of paper).

**Language support:**

- *gain* = get more; *lose* = have fewer.
- *neutral* = the same amount of + and −.

**Differentiation:**

- Support: a table with columns "object / gains or loses electrons / charge".
- Stretch: why does a charged balloon stick to a neutral wall? (The charges in the wall move a little: the near side gets the opposite charge.)

**Safety:** do not use charged objects near flammable vapours. Van de Graaff generators: follow school rules (not for students with heart conditions).

</Step>
