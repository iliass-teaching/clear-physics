<Step kind="learn" title="Conductors and insulators" minutes={12}>

- An **electrical conductor** lets charge flow through it easily. Examples: **metals** (copper, aluminium, silver), graphite (carbon).
- An **electrical insulator** does not let charge flow through it. Examples: **plastic**, **rubber**, **glass**, **dry wood**, air.

**The electron model:**

<Figure svg={conductorsSvg()} caption="Electrons in a conductor and in an insulator." width="32rem" />

- In a **metal**, some electrons are **free**: they are not held by one atom, so they can move through the whole metal. They carry the charge.
- In an **insulator**, all the electrons are **held** by their atoms. They cannot move through the material.

This is why you can charge an insulator by friction (the charge stays where it is), but a metal rod held in your hand does not stay charged (the electrons flow away through it and through you).

</Step>

<Step kind="do" title="Test the materials" minutes={15}>

**Equipment:** a cell (or a low-voltage power supply), a lamp, three leads, two crocodile clips, materials to test: a copper wire, an iron nail, a plastic ruler, a pencil lead (graphite), a rubber band, a coin, glass, a piece of wood, an aluminium foil.

1. Build a circuit with a gap between two crocodile clips.
2. Connect each material across the gap.
3. If the lamp lights, the material is a **conductor**. If not, it is an **insulator**.
4. Record your results in a table.

**Another method:** touch the cap of a charged electroscope with the material held in your hand. If the leaf falls, the charge flowed away: the material is a conductor.

</Step>

<Step kind="learn" title="Electric fields (Extended)" minutes={12}>

An **electric field** is a **region** in which an **electric charge feels a force**.

The **direction** of the field at a point is the direction of the **force on a positive charge** at that point. So field lines go **away from** positive charges and **towards** negative charges.

<Figure svg={electricFieldsSvg()} caption="Three electric field patterns." width="38rem" />

- Around a **point charge**, and around a **charged conducting sphere**: the lines are **radial** (like the spokes of a wheel), going out from + or in to −.
- Between two oppositely charged **parallel plates**: the lines are **straight, parallel and equally spaced** (a uniform field), from + to −.

</Step>

<Step kind="teacher" title="Teacher notes">

**Core and Extended:** conductors and insulators (4.2.1 C5, C6) are Core. Electric fields (S8–S10) are Extended. Core students can do a second round of the circuit test with more materials instead.

**Timing:** starter 5 min, learn 12 min, test 15 min, electric fields 12 min, check 11 min, exit ticket 5 min.

**Common mistakes:**

- "Insulators have no electrons."
- "In a wire, electrons travel very fast from the battery to the lamp." (They drift slowly; this comes in Unit 13.)
- Electric field arrows from − to +.

**Language support:**

- *conduct* = let something go through. The same word was used for thermal energy in Unit 8.
- *radial* = going out from a centre, like the spokes of a bicycle wheel.

**Differentiation:**

- Support: a results table to complete with "lamp on / off".
- Stretch: water from the tap conducts a little. Why is pure water a poor conductor? (It has very few ions.)

**Safety:** use only cells or low-voltage supplies. Never test materials with mains electricity.

</Step>
