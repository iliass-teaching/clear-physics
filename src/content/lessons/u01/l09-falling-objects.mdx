<Step kind="learn" title="Falling with no air resistance" minutes={10}>

Near the surface of the Earth, every object falls with the same acceleration, **if there is no air resistance**.
This is the **acceleration of free fall**, with the symbol $g$:

$$
g \approx 9.8\,\text{m/s}^2
$$

$g$ is (approximately) constant. So the speed of a falling object increases by about 9.8 m/s **every second**.

|---|---|---|---|---|
| speed / (m/s) | 0 | 9.8 | 19.6 | 29.4 |

</div>

In 1971, an astronaut on the Moon dropped a hammer and a feather together. There is no air on the Moon. They landed **at the same time**.

On Earth, the paper in the starter falls slowly because of **air resistance**. On top of the book, the air does not push on it, so it falls with the book.

<WorkedExample
  question="Extended: a stone falls from rest. Ignore air resistance. Calculate its speed after 2.5 s."
  given={['$g = 9.8\\,\\text{m/s}^2$', 'start speed $= 0\\,\\text{m/s}$', 'time $\\Delta t = 2.5\\,\\text{s}$']}
  find="the speed after 2.5 s"
  answer="$24.5\,\text{m/s}$"
>

$a = \dfrac{\Delta v}{\Delta t}$, so $\Delta v = a \times \Delta t$

$\Delta v = 9.8 \times 2.5 = 24.5\,\text{m/s}$

It starts from rest, so the speed is $24.5\,\text{m/s}$.

</WorkedExample>

</Step>

<Step kind="learn" title="Falling with air resistance (Extended)" minutes={10}>

In air, a falling object feels **air resistance**. Air resistance gets bigger when the speed gets bigger.

1. **At the start**, the speed is low. Air resistance is small. The object accelerates at about 9.8 m/s².
2. **As it falls faster**, air resistance increases. The acceleration gets smaller.
3. **In the end**, air resistance is as big as the weight. The forces balance. The acceleration is zero, so the speed stays constant.

This constant top speed is the **terminal velocity**.

<Graph
  xLabel="time / s" yLabel="speed / (m/s)" xMax={14} yMax={60} xStep={2} yStep={10}
  points={[[0, 0], [2, 18], [4, 32], [6, 42], [8, 48], [10, 51], [12, 52.5], [14, 53]]} curve
  caption="A skydiver: the curve gets less steep, then flat at terminal velocity."
/>

When the skydiver opens the parachute, air resistance becomes much bigger. The skydiver slows down to a new, **lower** terminal velocity, and can land safely.

</Step>

<Step kind="teacher" title="Teacher notes">

**Core and Extended:** $g \approx 9.8\,\text{m/s}^2$ and constant (1.2 C8) is Core. Falling with air resistance and terminal velocity (1.2 S13) is Extended. The calculations use $a = \dfrac{\Delta v}{\Delta t}$ (1.2 S9, Extended).

**Timing:** starter 5 min, learn 20 min, practical 15 min, practice 15 min, exit ticket 5 min.

**Common mistakes:**

- "Heavy objects fall faster." True in air for objects of the same shape, but not in free fall.
- Thinking the object stops at terminal velocity.
- Thinking the skydiver goes up when the parachute opens (the skydiver slows down; still moving down).
- Unit of $g$ written as m/s.

**Language support:**

- Act out the three stages of the fall with your hand (fast, slower, constant).
- Key sentence to learn: *"Air resistance increases until it is equal to the weight."*

**Differentiation:**

- Support: focus on the table and the straight-line graph of free fall.
- Stretch: why does a stack of 4 muffin cases have a higher terminal velocity than 1 case? (Same shape, so similar air resistance at the same speed, but 4 times the weight: it must fall faster before air resistance balances the weight.)

**Note:** forces and resultant force are taught in Unit 3. Here, "the forces balance" is enough.

**Safety:** for the muffin cases, stand on the floor. Do not climb on chairs or tables.

</Step>
