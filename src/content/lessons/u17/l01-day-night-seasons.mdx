<Step kind="starter" title="Does the Sun move?" minutes={5}>

In the morning, the Sun is in the east. In the evening, it is in the west.

Does the Sun move around the Earth? Or is something else happening? Talk with a partner.

</Step>

<Step kind="learn" title="Day and night" minutes={10}>

The Earth **rotates** (spins) around its **axis** once in about **24 hours**.

- The half of the Earth that faces the Sun has **day**.
- The other half has **night**.

<Figure svg={dayNightSvg()} caption="The Earth spins. The side facing the Sun has day." width="30rem" />

The Earth spins from west to east. So, for us on the Earth, the Sun **seems** to rise in the east, move across the sky, and set in the west.
The Sun does not go around the Earth: **we** turn.

</Step>

<Step kind="learn" title="The seasons" minutes={12}>

The Earth **orbits** the Sun once in about **365 days** (one year).

The Earth’s axis is **tilted** by about $23.5^\circ$. It always points in the same direction in space.

<Figure svg={seasonsSvg()} caption="In June and in December, the axis points the same way, but the Earth is on the other side of the Sun." width="34rem" />

**In June**, the northern **hemisphere** is tilted towards the Sun:

- the Sun is higher in the sky;
- the days are longer;
- the sunlight is more concentrated: each square metre of ground gets more energy.

So it is **summer** in the north. At the same time, the southern hemisphere is tilted away: it is winter there.

**In December**, it is the opposite: winter in the north, summer in the south.

**Be careful:** the seasons do **not** come from the distance to the Sun. The Earth is closest to the Sun in early January, during the northern winter.

</Step>

<Step kind="do" title="Model the Earth and the Sun" minutes={15}>

**Equipment:** a lamp (the Sun), a ball with a stick through it (the Earth and its axis), a torch, squared paper.

**Part 1 — Day and night**

1. Put the lamp in the middle of the table. Switch off the room lights.
2. Hold the ball near the lamp. Spin it slowly around the stick.
3. Put a sticker on the ball. When is it day for the sticker? When is it night?

**Part 2 — The seasons**

1. Tilt the stick a little. Keep it pointing at the same wall all the time.
2. Walk the ball slowly around the lamp.
3. Which half of the ball gets more light on the left of the lamp? On the right?

**Part 3 — Why summer is warmer**

1. Hold the torch straight above the squared paper. Draw around the bright spot.
2. Now hold the torch at a low angle, at the same distance. Draw around the spot again.
3. Count the squares in each spot. In which spot is the light more concentrated?

*Start your answer like this: "When the light comes from a low angle, the same energy is spread over …"*

</Step>

<Step kind="teacher" title="Teacher notes">

**Timing:** starter 5 min, learn 22 min, model 15 min, check 13 min, exit ticket 5 min.

**Common mistakes:**

- "Summer is when the Earth is closer to the Sun." The Earth is closest to the Sun in early January.
- "The Sun goes around the Earth." Use the model to show that the spinning Earth makes the Sun seem to move.
- Mixing up the spin (24 hours: day and night) and the orbit (365 days: the seasons).

**Language support:**

- Act out *rotate* (spin on the spot) and *orbit* (walk around a chair) before the lesson.
- Sentence frame: *"In June, the … hemisphere is tilted towards the Sun, so …"*

**Differentiation:**

- Support: start with Part 1 of the model only.
- Stretch: why are the seasons very small near the equator? (The Sun is high in the sky all year.)

**Safety:** do not touch the hot lamp. Never look directly at the Sun.

</Step>
