<Step kind="learn" title="What keeps the planets in orbit?" minutes={10}>

The **Sun** contains **most of the mass** of the Solar System (more than 99%).
So the Sun’s gravity is very strong, and the planets orbit the Sun.

The force that keeps a planet in its orbit is the **gravitational attraction of the Sun**. It acts like the string in the starter: it pulls the planet towards the Sun all the time.
Without this force, the planet would move away in a straight line.

</Step>

<Step kind="learn" title="Gravity of a planet" minutes={8}>

The **gravitational field strength** $g$ tells us the gravitational force on each kilogram.

- **At the surface** of a planet, $g$ depends on the **mass** of the planet: a planet with more mass usually has a stronger field.
- **Around** a planet, $g$ gets **smaller** when the distance from the planet gets bigger.

<PlanetTable columns={['diameter', 'g']} />

Your mass is the same on every planet. But your weight (the gravitational force on you) changes: on Mars, you weigh less than half your weight on the Earth.

</Step>

<Step kind="learn" title="Elliptical orbits (Extended)" minutes={10}>

The orbits of planets, minor planets and comets are **elliptical** (a stretched circle).
The Sun is **not** at the centre of the ellipse. (For an orbit that is almost a circle, the Sun is almost at the centre. The orbits of most planets are almost circles.)

<Figure svg={ellipticalOrbitSvg()} caption="An elliptical orbit. The shape is exaggerated: the orbits of most planets are almost circles." width="30rem" />

An object in an elliptical orbit moves **faster when it is closer to the Sun**. We can explain this with the **conservation of energy**:

- When the object moves **towards** the Sun, it loses gravitational potential energy and gains kinetic energy: it speeds up.
- When it moves **away** from the Sun, it loses kinetic energy and gains gravitational potential energy: it slows down.

The Sun’s gravitational field also gets weaker further from the Sun. So the planets far from the Sun move more slowly:

<Step kind="teacher" title="Teacher notes">

**Core and Extended:** 6.1.2 C3, C5 and C6 are Core. Elliptical orbits (S7), orbital speed and distance (S9), and the energy explanation (S10) are Extended.

**Timing:** starter 5 min, learn 28 min, data task 12 min, check 10 min, exit ticket 5 min.

**Common mistakes:**

- "There is no gravity in space." There is: it gets weaker with distance, but it keeps the planets in orbit.
- "The Sun is at the centre of every orbit." Only for orbits that are almost circles.
- Confusing mass and weight.

**Data note:** $g$ at the surface does not depend on mass only: it also depends on the size of the planet. The syllabus only asks for the link with mass. For the gas planets, NASA gives $g$ at the cloud tops (where the pressure is 1 bar).

**Homework answers:** Earth 490 N, Mars 185 N, Jupiter 1155 N. $W = mg$ is from Unit 2.

**Language support:**

- Sentence frame: *"When the planet moves towards the Sun, it loses … and gains …, so it …"*

**Differentiation:**

- Support: focus on the ball-on-a-string model and the Core questions.
- Stretch: Halley’s comet has a very elliptical orbit. Sketch it and mark where it is fastest and slowest.

**Safety:** use a soft ball and keep a clear space around you for the starter demonstration.

</Step>
