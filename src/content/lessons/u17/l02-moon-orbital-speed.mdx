<Step kind="learn" title="The phases of the Moon" minutes={12}>

The Moon **orbits** the Earth in about **one month**.

We see the Moon because it **reflects** light from the Sun.
The Sun always lights **half** of the Moon. But as the Moon orbits the Earth, we see a different part of this lit half. These shapes are the **phases of the Moon**.

<Figure svg={moonPhasesSvg()} caption="Top: the Moon at four points of its orbit. Bottom: what we see from the Earth (northern hemisphere)." width="34rem" />

- **New moon:** the Moon is between the Earth and the Sun. We see the dark half.
- **First quarter:** we see half of the lit half: the right side.
- **Full moon:** the Earth is between the Moon and the Sun. We see all of the lit half.
- **Last quarter:** we see the left side.

Then the cycle starts again. From one new moon to the next takes about 29.5 days.

</Step>

<Step kind="learn" title="Orbital speed (Extended)" minutes={10}>

In one orbit, the Moon goes around a circle. The distance for one orbit is the circumference: $2\pi r$, where $r$ is the radius of the orbit.
The time for one orbit is the **orbital period** $T$.

$$
\text{speed} = \frac{\text{distance}}{\text{time}}
\qquad\text{so}\qquad
v = \frac{2\pi r}{T}
$$

<WorkedExample
  question="The Moon orbits the Earth in 27.3 days. The radius of its orbit is 3.84 × 10⁸ m. Calculate its average orbital speed."
  given={['$r = 3.84 \\times 10^{8}\\,\\text{m}$', '$T = 27.3$ days']}
  find="the average orbital speed $v$"
  answer="$v = 1.02 \times 10^{3}\,\text{m/s}$ (about 1 km/s)"
>

Change the period to seconds: $T = 27.3 \times 24 \times 3600 = 2.36 \times 10^{6}\,\text{s}$

$v = \dfrac{2\pi r}{T} = \dfrac{2\pi \times 3.84 \times 10^{8}}{2.36 \times 10^{6}}$

$v = 1.02 \times 10^{3}\,\text{m/s}$

</WorkedExample>

**Tip:** always change the period to **seconds** first.

</Step>

<Step kind="do" title="Make the phases" minutes={15}>

**Equipment:** a lamp (the Sun), a white ball on a stick (the Moon). Your head is the Earth.

1. Switch off the room lights. Stand a few metres from the lamp.
2. Hold the ball at arm’s length, in front of your face.
3. Turn slowly on the spot (anticlockwise), so the ball goes around your head.
4. Watch the lit part of the ball. Draw what you see at 4 positions.
5. Label your drawings: new moon, first quarter, full moon, last quarter.

**Question:** where is the ball when you see a full moon?
*Start your answer like this: "I see a full moon when the ball is …"*

</Step>

<Step kind="teacher" title="Teacher notes">

**Core and Extended:** the Moon’s orbit and phases (6.1.1 C3) are Core. The orbital speed equation (6.1.1 S4) is Extended.

**Timing:** starter 5 min, learn 22 min, model 15 min, practice 13 min, exit ticket 5 min.

**Common mistakes:**

- "The phases are the shadow of the Earth." The shadow of the Earth only gives a lunar eclipse.
- Forgetting $2\pi$, or using $\pi r$.
- Not changing days or hours to seconds.

**Background:** the Moon takes 27.3 days to orbit the Earth, but 29.5 days from one new moon to the next, because the Earth also moves around the Sun. The syllabus only needs "about one month".

**Language support:**

- Teach *reflect* with a mirror and a torch.
- Sentence frame: *"At full moon, the … is between the … and the …"*

**Differentiation:**

- Support: Core students can do Level 1 of the practice with a calculator guide for $2\pi$.
- Stretch: the phases look the other way round in the southern hemisphere. Why?

</Step>
