<Step kind="starter" title="Ice in a pan" minutes={5}>

You heat a pan of ice on a cooker. A thermometer is in the ice. The ice starts to melt. The cooker still gives energy. Predict: what does the thermometer show while the ice melts?

</Step>

<Step kind="learn" title="A heating curve" minutes={14}>

We heat some ice at a steady rate and read its temperature every 30 seconds:

<Graph
  xLabel="time / min" yLabel="temperature / °C"
  xMax={20} yMin={-20} yMax={120} xStep={2} yStep={20}
  points={[[0, -20], [2, 0], [6, 0], [14, 100], [20, 100]]}
  labels={[{ x: 4, y: 8, text: 'melting' }, { x: 17, y: 108, text: 'boiling' }]}
  title="Heating curve of water: the temperature rises, stays at 0 °C while the ice melts, rises again, then stays at 100 °C while the water boils."
  caption="Heating curve of water. The flat parts: melting and boiling."
/>

- **0–2 min:** ice gets hotter.
- **2–6 min:** the ice **melts**. The temperature stays at **0 °C**, but the ice still gets energy.
- **6–14 min:** the water gets hotter.
- **14–20 min:** the water **boils**. The temperature stays at **100 °C**.

**Where does the energy go when the temperature does not change?** It is used to **separate the particles**: to break them out of their fixed places (melting), or to pull them far apart (boiling). The internal energy goes up, but the temperature does not.

For **water**, at normal air pressure:

- **melting point** (and freezing point): **0 °C**
- **boiling point**: **100 °C**

</Step>

<Step kind="learn" title="Condensation and solidification" minutes={8}>

When we cool a substance, the changes go the other way, and **energy is given out**.

- **Condensation (gas → liquid):** the gas particles lose energy and move more slowly. They come close together and the forces between them hold them together: they form a liquid.
- **Solidification (liquid → solid):** the liquid particles lose energy. They stop moving around each other and settle into fixed places in a regular pattern: they form a solid.

During condensation and solidification, the temperature also stays the same until the change is finished.

</Step>

<Step kind="do" title="Measure a cooling curve" minutes={15}>

**Equipment:** a test tube of stearic acid (or another solid that melts at about 70 °C), a beaker of hot water, a thermometer, a stopwatch.

1. Melt the stearic acid in the hot water. Put the thermometer in it.
2. Take the tube out of the water. Read the temperature every 30 seconds for 10 minutes.
3. Plot a graph: temperature (vertical axis) against time (horizontal axis).

**Questions:**

1. Where is the graph flat? What happens in the tube at this time?
2. What is the melting point of the substance?
3. The tube gives out energy to the air during the flat part. Why does the temperature not go down?

</Step>

<Step kind="teacher" title="Teacher notes">

**Timing:** starter 5 min, learn 22 min, cooling curve 15 min, check 13 min, exit ticket 5 min.

**Syllabus note:** specific latent heat is not in this syllabus. Keep to "energy input without a change in temperature".

**Practical:** stearic acid (melting point about 69 °C) or salol (about 42 °C) gives a clear flat section. Use a water bath, not a direct flame. If no chemicals are available, give a table of results and ask students to plot it.

**Common mistakes:**

- "During boiling, the temperature keeps rising."
- "The energy disappears during melting."
- Mixing up *boiling point* in °C (100) and in K (373).

**Language support:**

- *solidification* = *freezing*. Both words are used in exam papers.
- Sentence frame: *"Between … and … minutes, the line is flat because the substance is … . The energy is used to … ."*

**Differentiation:**

- Support: give the heating curve with the five parts labelled A to E, and sentences to match.
- Stretch: why does pasta cook faster in a pressure cooker? (At higher pressure, water boils at a higher temperature.)

**Safety:** hot water and hot test tubes. Stearic acid: wear eye protection.

</Step>
