<Step kind="starter" title="Through empty space" minutes={5}>

Between the Sun and the Earth, there is almost nothing: a vacuum. There are no particles to conduct, and no fluid for convection. So how does the Sun's energy reach us?

</Step>

<Step kind="learn" title="Infrared radiation" minutes={10}>

- **Thermal radiation** is **infrared** radiation: an electromagnetic wave, like light, but we cannot see it. We can feel it on our skin.
- **All objects emit** infrared radiation, even cold ones. Hotter objects emit more.
- Thermal radiation **does not need a medium** (a material to travel through). It can travel through a **vacuum**. This is how the Sun's energy reaches the Earth.
- It travels at the speed of light.

</Step>

<Step kind="learn" title="Colour and texture" minutes={12}>

<div class="table-wrap">

| Surface | Emitting (giving out) | Absorbing (taking in) | Reflecting |
|---|---|---|---|
| **matt (dull) black** | best | best | worst |
| **shiny silver** (or white) | worst | worst | best |

</div>

- **Black** is a better emitter and absorber than **white**.
- **Matt (dull)** is a better emitter and absorber than **shiny**.
- A good **absorber** is also a good **emitter**.

**In everyday life:**

- Houses in hot countries are often painted **white**: they reflect the Sun's radiation.
- Solar water heaters have **black** pipes: they absorb the most radiation.
- Emergency blankets for runners are **shiny**: they reflect the body's radiation back and reduce energy loss.

</Step>

<Step kind="learn" title="Experiments (Extended)" minutes={12}>

**Good and bad emitters: the Leslie cube**

<Figure svg={leslieCubeSvg()} caption="A Leslie cube, seen from above. Each face has a different surface." width="28rem" />

1. Fill the cube with very hot water. All the faces are at the same temperature.
2. Put the infrared detector at the **same distance** from each face, in turn.
3. Read the detector. The **matt black** face gives the highest reading; the **shiny silver** face gives the lowest.

**Good and bad absorbers**

<Figure svg={absorbersSvg()} caption="Two plates at the same distance from a heater." width="28rem" />

1. Put a matt black plate and a shiny plate at the **same distance** from a heater.
2. Fix a coin (or a pin) on the back of each plate with a little wax.
3. Switch on the heater. The wax on the **matt black** plate melts first: it absorbs more radiation.

**What else changes the rate of emission?**

- **Surface temperature:** a hotter surface emits radiation at a **higher rate**.
- **Surface area:** a bigger surface emits radiation at a **higher rate**. This is why radiators and cooling fins have a big area.

</Step>

<Step kind="teacher" title="Teacher notes">

**Core and Extended:** 2.3.3 C1–C3 are Core. The experiments (S7, S8) and the effect of temperature and area (S9) are Extended. Energy balance (S4–S6) is in the next lesson.

**Timing:** starter 5 min, learn 34 min, check 13 min, exit ticket 5 min. Show the Leslie cube as a demonstration if possible.

**About real results (for the teacher):** in a real Leslie cube experiment, the big difference is between **painted or dull** faces and **polished metal** faces. At these temperatures, white paint emits infrared almost as well as black paint (emissivity tables give values of about 0.9 for most paints, whatever their colour, and much lower values for polished metals). The syllabus and the exam expect "black is better than white" and "matt is better than shiny". This is true for absorbing sunlight (visible light), and it is the expected answer. If your results show matt white close to matt black, it is a good discussion for strong students.

**Common mistakes:**

- "Only hot objects emit radiation."
- "Shiny surfaces absorb radiation and make things hot."
- Confusing thermal radiation with radioactivity (Unit 16).

**Language support:**

- *emit* = give out; *absorb* = take in; *reflect* = send back.
- *matt* = dull, not shiny (also written *matte*).

**Differentiation:**

- Support: a three-column sorting task: emit / absorb / reflect, with pictures.
- Stretch: why do cooling fins on a motorbike engine have many thin plates? (Bigger surface area: more radiation and more convection.)

**Safety:** the Leslie cube is full of very hot water. Fill it on a heatproof mat and do not move it.

</Step>
