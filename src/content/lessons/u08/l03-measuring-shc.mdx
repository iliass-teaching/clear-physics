<Step kind="starter" title="Plan it" minutes={5}>

You have a 1 kg metal block. You want to find its specific heat capacity: $c = \dfrac{\Delta E}{m \Delta\theta}$.

What three quantities must you measure? Which instrument measures each one?

</Step>

<Step kind="learn" title="The method for a solid" minutes={12}>

<Figure svg={shcSetupSvg()} caption="Measuring the specific heat capacity of a metal block." width="32rem" />

1. Measure the **mass** $m$ of the block with a balance.
2. Put the **heater** and the **thermometer** in the two holes. Put a drop of oil in the thermometer hole: it helps the energy reach the thermometer.
3. Put **insulation** around the block.
4. Read the start temperature. Switch on the heater and start the stopwatch.
5. After about 10 minutes, switch off the heater. Read the **energy** $\Delta E$ on the joulemeter.
6. Wait. The temperature still goes up a little. Read the **highest** temperature.
7. Calculate $\Delta\theta$, then $c = \dfrac{\Delta E}{m \Delta\theta}$.

**No joulemeter?** Use the power of the heater: $\Delta E = P t$, with $t$ in seconds.

**For a liquid** (for example water): put a known mass of water in an insulated beaker. Use an immersion heater. **Stir** the water before each reading, so all of it is at the same temperature.

</Step>

<Step kind="do" title="Virtual practical" minutes={20}>

<ShcSim />

1. Choose **aluminium**, with insulation. Record a reading at the start.
2. Switch on the heater. Record a reading every 2 minutes (on the clock) until 10 minutes. Then switch off.
3. Use your first and last readings to calculate $c$. Check your value.
4. Repeat **without insulation**. Is your value better or worse? Why?
5. If you have time: measure $c$ for water.

</Step>

<Step kind="learn" title="Why is the value too high?" minutes={5}>

Some energy from the heater **does not stay in the block**: it goes to the surroundings (the air, the table).

- So the temperature rise $\Delta\theta$ is **smaller** than it should be.
- $\Delta\theta$ is at the bottom of the fraction, so the value of $c$ is **too big**.

**To improve:** use insulation; stir liquids; put oil in the thermometer hole; start a little below room temperature (optional).

</Step>

<Step kind="teacher" title="Teacher notes">

**Extended only** (2.2.2 S4). Core students can use the simulation to see that different materials heat up at different rates, without the calculation.

**Timing:** starter 5 min, method 12 min, simulation 20 min, discussion 5 min, practice 13 min, exit ticket 5 min.

**Real practical (if available):** 1 kg aluminium blocks with two holes, 12 V heaters, joulemeters (or a power supply, ammeter, voltmeter and stopwatch; the electrical energy equation comes in Unit 13). Typical results with 50 W for 10 minutes: a rise of about 30 °C for aluminium.

**About the simulation:** the model uses a 50 W heater and simple energy losses (bigger without insulation). The clock runs 30 times faster. Students should find values a few percent above the data-book value with insulation, and clearly higher without it.

**Common mistakes:**

- Reading the temperature as soon as the heater is switched off. The temperature still rises: the heater is hotter than the block.
- Time in minutes in $\Delta E = Pt$.
- Saying "the energy is destroyed". It is transferred to the surroundings.

**Safety:** the heater and the block get hot. Do not touch them for several minutes after switching off. Keep water away from the electrical connections.

</Step>
