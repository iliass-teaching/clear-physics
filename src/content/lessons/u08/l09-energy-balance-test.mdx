<div class="table-wrap">

| | How does the energy move? | Where? |
|---|---|---|
| **conduction** | from particle to particle (and by free electrons in metals) | mainly solids |
| **convection** | the hot fluid moves and carries the energy | liquids and gases |
| **radiation** | infrared waves | everywhere, even in a vacuum |

</div>

**Extended: when more than one type matters**

- **A fire burning wood or coal:** you feel the **radiation** on your face. Hot gases rise up the chimney by **convection**, and cool air comes in at the bottom to keep the fire burning. The grate and the bricks heat up by **conduction**.
- **A car radiator:** water carries energy from the hot engine to the radiator (convection, helped by a pump). The energy passes through the thin metal walls and fins by **conduction**. Air flowing through the radiator carries it away by **convection** (helped by a fan). The radiator also emits some **radiation**, but most of the energy leaves by convection.

</Step>

<Step kind="learn" title="Energy balance (Extended)" minutes={12}>

An object **receives** energy (for example from the Sun) and **transfers** energy away (for example by radiation).

- If it receives energy **at the same rate** as it transfers energy away, its temperature stays **constant**.
- If it receives energy **faster** than it transfers it away, its temperature **goes up**. A hotter object emits faster, so it soon reaches a new balance at a higher temperature.
- If it receives energy **more slowly** than it transfers it away, its temperature **goes down**.

<Figure svg={earthBalanceSvg()} caption="The Earth receives radiation from the Sun and emits infrared radiation into space." width="30rem" />

**The Earth:**

- The Earth receives radiation from the Sun. It emits infrared radiation into space.
- **Greenhouse gases** in the atmosphere (for example carbon dioxide, methane, water vapour) absorb some of the infrared from the Earth and emit some of it back towards the surface.
- More greenhouse gases: less energy escapes, so the Earth's temperature goes up until a new balance is reached. This is **global warming**.
- Other factors: **clouds**, **ice and snow** (they reflect sunlight), and the energy from the Sun.

</Step>

<Step kind="teacher" title="Teacher notes">

**Core and Extended:** everyday applications (2.3.4 C1) are Core. Fires and car radiators (2.3.4 S2) and energy balance (2.3.3 S4–S6) are Extended.

**Timing:** review 10 min, energy balance 12 min, quick check 5 min, unit test 33 min. Core students can use the energy-balance time to revise lessons 1–8.

**Using the test:** questions 1–7 test Core content; 8–10 are Extended.

**About the greenhouse effect:** keep to the syllabus idea of balance between incoming and outgoing radiation. Avoid the common wrong model "greenhouse gases reflect heat like a mirror". They absorb infrared and emit it again in all directions, including back towards the Earth.

**Common mistakes:**

- "A car radiator works mainly by radiation." Most of the energy leaves by convection.
- "Constant temperature means no energy transfer." It means energy in = energy out.

**Differentiation:**

- Support: before the test, give the three-column table (conduction / convection / radiation) to complete.
- Stretch: a vacuum flask has a vacuum between two glass walls, and shiny walls. Explain how it reduces conduction, convection and radiation.

</Step>
