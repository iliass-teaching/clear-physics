<Step kind="learn" title="Rules for parallel circuits" minutes={15}>

<Figure svg={seriesParallelSvg()} caption="A parallel circuit (right) has two or more branches." width="34rem" />

In a **parallel circuit**, the current splits into **branches**:

1. The **current from the source** is **bigger** than the current in each branch.
2. The **combined resistance** of two resistors in parallel is **less** than either resistor alone. (There are more paths for the current.)
3. If one branch breaks, the other branches **still work**.

**Advantages of lamps in parallel in a lighting circuit:**

- each lamp can be switched **on and off on its own**;
- if one lamp breaks, the **others stay on**;
- each lamp gets the **full p.d.** of the supply, so it is **bright**.

**Extended: two important rules**

<Figure svg={junctionSvg()} caption="The current into a junction equals the current out of it." width="28rem" />

- **Junction rule:** the sum of the currents **into** a junction = the sum of the currents **out of** it. Charge is not lost or created: every coulomb that arrives must leave.
- **Same p.d.:** the p.d. across each branch is the **same** (and equal to the p.d. across the whole arrangement).

</Step>

<Step kind="teacher" title="Teacher notes">

**Core and Extended:** 4.3.2 C5–C7 are Core. The junction rule and equal p.d.s (S8a, S8c, S9) are Extended.

**Timing:** starter 5 min, learn 15 min, simulation 15 min, practice 18 min, exit ticket 7 min.

**Common mistakes:**

- "In parallel, the current is the same in each branch." (Only if the branches have the same resistance.)
- "Adding a resistor in parallel increases the total resistance." It decreases it.
- Forgetting that the supply current increases when more branches are added.

**Language support:**

- *parallel*: side by side, like the lines of an equals sign (=).
- *branch*: like the branches of a tree.

**Differentiation:**

- Support: level 1 of the practice only.
- Stretch: why does a battery go flat faster with lamps in parallel than in series?

</Step>
