Radiation can cause cancer, and radiation can treat cancer. How can both be true?

</Step>

<Step kind="learn" title="Dangers and safety" minutes={14}>

**Effects on living things:** ionising radiation can damage the molecules in cells (including DNA). This can cause:

- **cell death**;
- **mutations** (changes in the DNA);
- **cancer**.

**Moving, using and storing radioactive materials safely:**

- Store sources in **lead-lined boxes**, in a locked cupboard, with a warning label.
- Move and handle them with **tongs** (never with your hands); point them **away** from people.
- Use them for the **shortest time** possible, and put them back at once.
- People who work with radiation wear a **badge** that measures their dose.

**Extended: three ways to reduce exposure**

- **Time:** a shorter time near the source means less radiation received.
- **Distance:** further from the source means much less radiation (it spreads out, and α and β are absorbed by air).
- **Shielding:** put an absorber (lead, concrete, water) between the source and people.

</Step>

<Step kind="learn" title="Uses: choosing the right isotope (Extended)" minutes={18}>

The choice depends on the **type of radiation** (how far it penetrates) and the **half-life** (how long the source lasts).

<div class="table-wrap">

| Use | Radiation | Why | Half-life |
|---|---|---|---|
| **smoke alarm** (for example americium-241) | α | α ionises the air between two plates: a small current flows. Smoke absorbs the α-particles: the current falls and the alarm sounds. α does not escape the alarm (stopped by a few cm of air). | **long** (hundreds of years), so the alarm works for many years |
| **irradiating food** to kill bacteria | γ | γ passes through the packaging and the food | long (the source is used for years) |
| **sterilising medical equipment** | γ | γ penetrates the sealed packets and kills bacteria | long |
| **measuring thickness** of paper, plastic or foil | β | some β passes through; the count changes when the thickness changes | long, so the count rate does not fall during use |
| thickness of **thick metal** | γ | β would be stopped completely | long |
| **diagnosis**: a tracer injected in the body (for example technetium-99m) | γ | γ escapes from the body and is detected by a camera | **short** (a few hours), so it does not stay active in the body |
| **treatment of cancer** | γ | high-energy beams from several directions meet at the tumour and kill the cancer cells | long for the machine source |

</div>

<Figure svg={thicknessGaugeSvg()} caption="Controlling the thickness of paper with a β source." width="32rem" />

**Why not α for thickness?** It would be stopped by any paper. **Why not γ for paper?** Almost all of it would pass through: the count would hardly change.

</Step>

<Step kind="teacher" title="Teacher notes">

**Core and Extended:** effects and safe handling (5.2.5 C1, C2) are Core. Choosing isotopes (5.2.4 S3) and time, distance, shielding (5.2.5 S3) are Extended.

**Timing:** starter 5 min, safety 14 min, uses 18 min, check 18 min, exit ticket 5 min.

**Facts used:** americium-241 (α, half-life 432 years) in smoke alarms; technetium-99m (γ, half-life 6.0 hours) as a medical tracer; cobalt-60 (γ, half-life 5.3 years) for sterilising and radiotherapy. These examples are common in textbooks; the syllabus does not require the names or values.

**Common mistakes:**

- "Irradiated food becomes radioactive." (It does not.)
- Choosing γ for a smoke alarm or α for a tracer.

**Language support:**

- *sterilise* = kill all bacteria; *tracer* = something we follow through the body.

**Differentiation:**

- Support: match each use to α, β or γ, with a reason card.
- Stretch: why does a cancer treatment beam come from several directions?

</Step>
