<Step kind="starter" title="A mystery box" minutes={5}>

You cannot open a box. You can only throw small balls into it through a hole and see where they come out. How could you find out what is inside?

</Step>

<Step kind="learn" title="The old model: the plum pudding" minutes={8}>

Before 1911, many scientists thought an atom was a ball of **positive** charge with small **negative** electrons inside it, like plums in a pudding. The positive charge and the mass were **spread out** through the whole atom.

</Step>

<Step kind="learn" title="The experiment (Extended)" minutes={12}>

In 1909, Hans Geiger and Ernest Marsden, working with Ernest Rutherford, fired **alpha particles** (small, fast, positive particles) at a very **thin gold foil**. A detector around the foil counted where the alpha particles went.

<Figure svg={alphaScatterSvg()} caption="Results of the alpha-particle scattering experiment." width="34rem" />

**Results and what they show:**

<div class="table-wrap">

| Result | Conclusion |
|---|---|
| **Most** α-particles went **straight through** | The atom is **mostly empty space**; the nucleus is **very small**. |
| **Some** were **deflected** (bent) | The nucleus is **positively charged**: it repels the positive α-particles. |
| **A very few bounced back** | The nucleus contains **most of the mass** of the atom (and its charge is concentrated in a tiny space). |

</div>

The plum pudding model could not explain the bouncing back: in that model, the forces are too small and too spread out. So Rutherford proposed the **nuclear model** (1911).

</Step>

<Step kind="do" title="Test both models" minutes={17}>

<AlphaSim />

1. With the **nuclear model**, fire 50 α-particles several times (at least 200 in total). Record how many go almost straight, are deflected, and bounce back.
2. Switch to the **plum pudding model**. Fire 200 α-particles. Record the results.
3. Which model agrees with the real experiment? Explain.

</Step>

<Step kind="teacher" title="Teacher notes">

**Extended only** (5.1.1 S3). Core students can use the simulation to see why the atom is "mostly empty space" (lesson 16.1).

**Timing:** starter 5 min, plum pudding 8 min, experiment 12 min, simulation 17 min, check 13 min, exit ticket 5 min.

**History:** the experiment was done by Geiger and Marsden (1909) under Rutherford's direction; Rutherford published the nuclear model in 1911.

**About the simulation:** one atom is shown, much enlarged, and the nucleus is drawn far bigger than its real size. So more α-particles are deflected than in the real experiment (Geiger and Marsden, 1909: with a platinum foil, about 1 in 8000 α-particles was turned back by more than 90°).

**Common mistakes:**

- "The α-particles bounced off electrons."
- "The atom is a solid ball."

**Differentiation:**

- Support: match the three results to the three conclusions.
- Stretch: why did the experiment need a very thin foil, and a vacuum?

</Step>
