<Step kind="starter" title="Three kinds" minutes={5}>

Radiation from radioactive materials is of more than one kind: some is stopped by a sheet of paper, some goes further. How could you test what stops each kind?

</Step>

<Step kind="learn" title="Three types of nuclear radiation" minutes={16}>

An unstable nucleus gives out radiation **spontaneously** (by itself, we cannot make it happen or stop it) and **randomly** in direction.

<div class="table-wrap">

| | α (alpha) | β (beta) | γ (gamma) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Nature** | a helium nucleus: 2 protons + 2 neutrons | a fast electron from the nucleus | an electromagnetic wave |
| **Charge** | +2 | −1 | 0 |
| **Ionising effect** | **strong** | medium | **weak** |
| **Stopped by** | a sheet of paper (or a few cm of air) | a few mm of aluminium | only reduced by several cm of lead (or thick concrete) |

</div>

<Figure svg={penetrationSvg()} caption="What stops each type of radiation." width="34rem" />

**Ionising** means knocking electrons off atoms, making ions. The **more ionising** a radiation is, the **faster it loses its energy**, so the **less penetrating** it is.

</Step>

<Step kind="learn" title="Deflection and ionisation (Extended)" minutes={14}>

<Figure svg={deflectionSvg()} caption="α, β and γ in an electric field and in a magnetic field." width="38rem" />

**Deflection:**

- **α** (positive) is deflected towards the **negative** plate in an electric field. It is heavy, so it is deflected only a **little**.
- **β** (negative) is deflected the **opposite way**, and **more** (it is very light).
- **γ** has **no charge**: it is **not deflected**.
- In a magnetic field, α and β curve in **opposite** directions (use the left-hand rule, with β as a current in the opposite direction to its motion); γ goes straight.

**Why is α so ionising?**

- It has a **big charge** (+2), so it pulls electrons off atoms strongly.
- It has a lot of **kinetic energy**, but it is heavy and slow, so it spends a long time near each atom it passes. It gives up its energy in a short distance.
- β has a smaller charge (−1) and moves much faster: it ionises less. γ has **no charge**: it ionises very little.

</Step>

<Step kind="teacher" title="Teacher notes">

**Core and Extended:** 5.2.2 C1, C2 are Core. Deflection (S3) and the explanation of ionising effects (S4) are Extended.

**Timing:** starter 5 min, learn 16 min, Extended 14 min, data task 10 min, check 10 min, exit ticket 5 min.

**Data task answers:** X: α (stopped by paper). Y: β (passes paper, stopped by aluminium). Z: γ (reduced by lead only). The fourth source emits α **and** β.

**Note:** in the syllabus, "β" means β⁻ (an electron); β⁺ is not included.

**Common mistakes:**

- "Gamma is the most dangerous because it is the most ionising." (It is the most penetrating, the least ionising.)
- Mixing up "ionising" and "penetrating".

**Language support:**

- *penetrate* = go through; *absorb* = take in and stop.

**Differentiation:**

- Support: a three-column card sort (α / β / γ).
- Stretch: inside the body, which radiation is the most harmful? Outside the body? Explain.

**Safety:** demonstrations with sealed sources only, by the teacher, following local rules.

</Step>
