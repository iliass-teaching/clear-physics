<Step kind="learn" title="Where does gas pressure come from?" minutes={10}>

<Figure svg={gasWallSvg()} caption="Each particle that hits the wall pushes on it." width="30rem" />

- The particles of a gas move fast, in random directions.
- They **collide** with the walls of the container and bounce back.
- Each collision **pushes** on the wall. Millions of collisions each second make a steady push: this is the **pressure** of the gas.

**Extended:** each collision exerts a small **force** on the wall. The total force on each unit of area is the pressure:

$$
p = \frac{F}{A}
$$

</Step>

<Step kind="learn" title="Changing the temperature or the volume" minutes={12}>

A **fixed mass of gas** is a gas in a closed container: no gas goes in or out.

**1. Heat the gas. Keep the volume the same.**

<Figure svg={gasCompareSvg('temperature')} caption="Hotter gas: the particles move faster." width="30rem" />

- The particles move **faster**.
- They hit the walls **more often** and **harder**.
- So the pressure **goes up**.

**2. Make the volume smaller. Keep the temperature the same.**

<Figure svg={gasCompareSvg('volume')} caption="Smaller volume: the particles hit the walls more often." width="30rem" />

- The particles move at the **same speed** (same temperature).
- But they have less space, so they hit the walls **more often**.
- So the pressure **goes up**.

**In real life:**

- After a long drive, the air in car tyres is warmer, so its pressure is higher. Check tyre pressure when the tyres are cold.
- A spray can says *do not heat*: hot gas inside could make the pressure too high, and the can could burst.

</Step>

<Step kind="teacher" title="Teacher notes">

**Core and Extended:** collisions with a surface (2.1.2 C3) and the two qualitative changes (2.1.3 C1) are Core. Pressure as force per unit area from collisions (2.1.2 S7) is Extended.

**Timing:** starter 5 min, learn 22 min, simulation 18 min, check 10 min, exit ticket 5 min.

**Expected results from the simulation:** at constant volume, the pressure is about proportional to the kelvin temperature (about 50 at 150 K, 100 at 300 K, 200 at 600 K). At constant temperature, halving the volume about doubles the pressure (about 200 at 50 %). The readings change a little because the motion is random: ask students to wait and take a typical value.

**Common mistakes:**

- "The particles move faster when the gas is compressed." Only if the temperature changes.
- "The particles expand when heated."
- "There are more particles in a smaller volume." There are the same number of particles, closer together.
- Forgetting the conditions: *at constant volume*, *at constant temperature*. Teach students to write them.

**Language support:**

- Sentence frame: *"When the … goes up, the particles … , so they hit the walls … . This means the pressure … ."*
- *More often* = more times each second. *Harder* = with more force.

**Differentiation:**

- Support: give the explanations as jumbled sentences to put in order.
- Stretch: in Part 1, plot pressure against temperature in kelvin. What shape is the graph? Where does it cross the temperature axis?

</Step>
