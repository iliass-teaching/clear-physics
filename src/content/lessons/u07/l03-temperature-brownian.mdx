<Step kind="learn" title="Temperature and the motion of particles" minutes={10}>

- When the temperature **goes up**, the particles move **faster**: they have more kinetic energy.
- When the temperature **goes down**, the particles move **slower**.
- There is a **lowest possible temperature**: **−273 °C**. It is called **absolute zero**. At absolute zero, the particles have the **least** kinetic energy possible. Nothing can be colder.

**The kelvin scale** starts at absolute zero. One kelvin (1 K) is the same size as one degree Celsius (1 °C).

<Figure svg={temperatureScalesSvg()} caption="The Celsius scale and the kelvin scale." width="24rem" />

$$
T\,(\text{in K}) = \theta\,(\text{in }^\circ\text{C}) + 273
$$

- To go from °C to K: **add 273**.
- To go from K to °C: **subtract 273**.
- We write **K**, not °K.

<WorkedExample
  question="Liquid nitrogen boils at 77 K. What is this temperature in °C?"
  given={['$T = 77\\,\\text{K}$']}
  find="the temperature $\theta$ in °C"
  answer="$\theta = -196\,^\circ\text{C}$"
>

$T = \theta + 273$, so $\theta = T - 273$

$\theta = 77 - 273 = -196\,^\circ\text{C}$

</WorkedExample>

</Step>

<Step kind="learn" title="Brownian motion" minutes={13}>

We cannot see the particles of a gas. So how do we know they move?

In 1827, the scientist Robert Brown looked at very small particles from pollen grains in water, with a microscope. They moved in a **random zigzag**, all the time. We can see the same motion with **smoke particles in air**.

<Figure svg={brownianSvg()} caption="In a microscope, each smoke particle moves in a random zigzag." width="20rem" />

**Explanation:**

- The air molecules are too small to see. They move **fast**, in **random** directions.
- They **collide** with the smoke particle from all sides.
- At each moment, more molecules hit one side than the other. So the smoke particle moves a little, then changes direction.

This motion is called **Brownian motion**. It is **evidence** for the **kinetic particle model**: matter is made of particles that move all the time.

**Extended:** the air molecules are **light** and **fast**. The smoke particle is much bigger and heavier, but many collisions with light, fast molecules can still move it. In your answers, use *molecules* (or *atoms*) for the air, and *microscopic particles* for the smoke.

</Step>

<Step kind="teacher" title="Teacher notes">

**Core and Extended:** temperature and motion, absolute zero, Brownian motion and the kelvin scale are Core. The idea of light, fast molecules moving heavier particles, and the correct use of *molecules* versus *microscopic particles* (2.1.2 S8), are Extended.

**Timing:** starter 5 min, learn 23 min, practice 12 min, simulation (or smoke cell) 15 min, exit ticket 5 min.

**Smoke cell demonstration (optional):** use a smoke cell with a microscope. Fill the cell with smoke from a burning straw, light it from the side and look through the microscope. Students see bright specks that move in a random zigzag. Follow your school's rules for flames and smoke.

**Common mistakes:**

- "The smoke particles move because they are hot" or "because they are alive".
- Calling the smoke particles *molecules*. They are much bigger: each one contains many molecules.
- "At absolute zero the particles stop." The syllabus says the particles have the *least* kinetic energy. Use this wording.
- Writing °K, or subtracting 273 to go from °C to K.

**About the simulation:** it is a 2D model with only 70 small particles, so the big particle moves more than a real smoke particle would. The small particles do not collide with each other in the model.

**Language support:**

- *Random* = no pattern; we cannot predict the next direction.
- *Evidence* = something we can see that shows an idea is true.

**Differentiation:**

- Support: give a number line from −273 °C to 100 °C with the kelvin values under it.
- Stretch: why does Brownian motion get faster when the temperature goes up?

</Step>
