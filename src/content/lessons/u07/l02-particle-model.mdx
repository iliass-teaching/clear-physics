<Step kind="learn" title="Everything is made of particles" minutes={15}>

All matter is made of very small **particles** (atoms or molecules). They are much too small to see, even with a normal microscope.

<Figure svg={statesSvg()} caption="Particles in a solid, a liquid and a gas." width="36rem" />

<div class="table-wrap">

| | Arrangement | Separation | Motion |
|---|---|---|---|
| **solid** | regular pattern | very close, touching | vibrate around fixed places |
| **liquid** | no regular pattern | close, touching | move around each other |
| **gas** | no pattern, random | far apart | move fast, in all directions |

</div>

**How to draw particle diagrams:**

- Draw all particles **the same size**: the particles do not change, only their arrangement.
- Solid: rows, circles touching. Liquid: circles touching, but no rows, with a few gaps. Gas: a few circles, far apart, with arrows.

</Step>

<Step kind="learn" title="Explaining the properties" minutes={10}>

<div class="table-wrap">

| Property | Explanation with particles |
|---|---|
| A solid has a fixed shape. | The particles stay in fixed places. |
| A liquid flows. | The particles can move around each other. |
| Solids and liquids are hard to compress. | The particles are already touching: there is no space between them. |
| A gas is easy to compress. | The particles are far apart: there is a lot of space between them. |
| A gas fills its container. | The particles move fast in all directions until they hit a wall. |

</div>

**Extended: forces between particles**

- There are forces between particles. In a solid, these forces are strong: they hold the particles in place.
- In a liquid, the particles are still close, but the forces cannot hold them in fixed places.
- In a gas, the particles are far apart, so the forces between them are very small (except when they collide).
- The **forces**, the **distances** between particles and the **motion** of the particles explain the properties of solids, liquids and gases.

</Step>

<Step kind="do" title="Model it" minutes={12}>

**Equipment:** a tray (or a Petri dish) and about 30 marbles or beads.

1. Pack the marbles tightly in one corner of the tray, in rows. Shake the tray **very gently**. This is a **solid**: the particles vibrate but stay in place.
2. Shake a little more. Some rows break, but the marbles still touch. This is a **liquid**.
3. Shake hard. The marbles move fast all over the tray and hit the walls. This is a **gas**.

**Draw** a particle diagram for each state. Label it with: *arrangement*, *separation*, *motion*.

**Think:** what does the shaking represent in real life? (Hint: what do we do to ice to make it melt?)

</Step>

<Step kind="teacher" title="Teacher notes">

**Core and Extended:** arrangement, separation and motion (2.1.2 C1) are Core. Forces and distances between particles (2.1.2 S6) are Extended.

**Timing:** starter 5 min, learn 25 min, model 12 min, check 13 min, exit ticket 5 min.

**Common mistakes:**

- Drawing bigger particles for a gas, or smaller ones for a solid. The particles are the same in all states.
- "The particles of a solid do not move." They vibrate.
- "The particles expand when heated." The particles do not change size; the spaces between them change (Unit 8, thermal expansion).
- Drawing a liquid with big gaps, like a gas. In a liquid, the particles touch.

**Exam tip:** the syllabus asks for *arrangement*, *separation* and *motion*. Teach students to answer with these three words every time.

**Language support:**

- Sentence frames: *"In a solid, the particles are … . They are … together. They … ."*
- *Separation* = *the distance between*. *Arrangement* = *how they are placed*.

**Differentiation:**

- Support: give dotted outlines for the particle diagrams.
- Stretch: water is unusual: ice floats on water, so ice is less dense than water. What does this tell you about the separation of the molecules in ice? (They are a little further apart than in liquid water.)

**Safety:** marbles on the floor are a slip hazard. Use trays with edges.

</Step>
