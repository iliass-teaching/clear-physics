<Step kind="starter" title="Is it work?" minutes={5}>

In physics, is work done in these cases? Yes or no?

1. You push a car, but it does not move.
2. You lift a bag from the floor onto a table.
3. You hold a heavy bag still for 5 minutes.
4. A crane lifts a beam.

</Step>

<Step kind="learn" title="Work done = energy transferred" minutes={12}>

**Work is done** when a force moves an object. The **work done** is the energy transferred:

$$
W = F \times d = \Delta E
$$

<div class="table-wrap">

| Quantity | Symbol | Unit |
|---|---|---|
| work done | $W$ | J |
| force | $F$ | N |
| distance moved (in the direction of the force) | $d$ | m |
| energy transferred | $\Delta E$ | J |

</div>

- If the object does not move ($d = 0$), **no work is done**, even if you get tired.
- 1 J of work is done when a force of 1 N moves an object 1 m.
- Electrical work is done when a current transfers energy in a circuit.

<WorkedExample
  question="A student pushes a box with a force of 120 N for 5.0 m. Calculate the work done."
  given={['$F = 120\\,\\text{N}$', '$d = 5.0\\,\\text{m}$']}
  find="the work done $W$"
  answer="$W = 600\,\text{J}$"
>

$W = F \times d = 120 \times 5.0$

$W = 600\,\text{J}$: 600 J of energy is transferred.

</WorkedExample>

</Step>

<Step kind="learn" title="Lifting" minutes={8}>

To lift an object at a steady speed, the force needed is equal to its **weight**: $F = mg$. So the work done is:

$$
W = mg \times \Delta h
$$

This is the same as the gain in gravitational potential energy: the work done is the energy transferred to the gravitational potential store.

Example: lifting a 5 kg bag 1.2 m: $W = 5 \times 9.8 \times 1.2 = 59\,\text{J}$.

</Step>

<Step kind="do" title="How much work?" minutes={12}>

Work in pairs with a force meter, a block with a hook and a metre rule.

1. Pull the block along the bench at a steady speed for 0.50 m. Read the force.
2. Calculate the work done.
3. Lift the block 0.50 m at a steady speed with the force meter. Read the force.
4. Calculate the work done.

**Question:** which needed more work? Why? Where did the energy go each time?

</Step>

<Step kind="teacher" title="Teacher notes">

**Timing:** starter 5 min, learn 20 min, activity 12 min, practice 18 min, exit ticket 5 min.

**Starter answers:** 1. no (no movement). 2. yes. 3. no (no movement). 4. yes.

**Common mistakes:**

- Using the mass (kg) as the force when lifting: the force is the weight, $mg$.
- Thinking work is done whenever a force acts.
- Confusing work done (J) and power (W) — the letter W is used for both work and the unit watt.

**Homework note:** the force on the bag is upwards, and the movement is horizontal. Only the distance in the direction of the force counts.

**Language support:**

- In everyday English, *work* means a job or effort. In physics, work is done only when a force moves something.

**Differentiation:**

- Support: start with Level 1 and simple whole numbers.
- Stretch: a 60 kg climber goes up 300 m. Calculate the work done against gravity in kJ. (176 kJ.)

</Step>
