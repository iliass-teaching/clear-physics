<Step kind="learn" title="How sound is made and how it travels" minutes={14}>

- Sound is made by **vibrating** objects: a guitar string, your vocal cords, a loudspeaker cone.
- The vibrating object pushes and pulls the air particles next to it. They push the next particles, and so on. The **energy** travels, but the air does not travel with it.
- The particles vibrate **along** the direction the sound travels: sound is a **longitudinal** wave.

<Figure svg={soundCompressionSvg()} caption="A loudspeaker makes compressions and rarefactions in the air." width="34rem" />

**Extended:**

- A **compression** is a region where the air particles are **close together** (higher pressure).
- A **rarefaction** is a region where the particles are **further apart** (lower pressure).

</Step>

<Step kind="learn" title="Key facts about sound" minutes={10}>

- **Human hearing:** about **20 Hz to 20 000 Hz** (20 kHz). Older people often cannot hear the highest frequencies.
- **Sound needs a medium** (a solid, a liquid or a gas). It **cannot** travel through a vacuum, so it cannot travel through space.
- **Speed of sound in air:** about **330–350 m/s** (it depends on the temperature). We often use 340 m/s.

**Extended: speed in different materials.** In general, sound travels faster in **solids** than in **liquids**, and faster in **liquids** than in **gases**:

<div class="table-wrap">

| Material | Speed of sound (about) |
|---|---|
| air | 340 m/s |
| water | 1500 m/s |
| steel | 5000–6000 m/s |

</div>

Compare light: $3.0 \times 10^8$ m/s, almost a million times faster than sound in air. This is why you see lightning before you hear the thunder.

</Step>

<Step kind="do" title="The bell in a jar" minutes={13}>

**Demonstration:** an electric bell rings inside a glass bell jar. A vacuum pump slowly removes the air.

1. Listen as the air is pumped out. What happens to the sound?
2. Can you still **see** the hammer hitting the bell? What does this tell you about light?
3. Let the air back in. What happens?

If you do not have the apparatus, watch a video of the experiment, and answer the same questions.

**Then:** put your ear on the table. A partner taps the other end gently. Is the sound louder through the table or through the air?

</Step>

<Step kind="teacher" title="Teacher notes">

**Core and Extended:** 3.4 C1–C5 are Core. Compression and rarefaction (S10) and the speed in solids, liquids and gases (S11) are Extended.

**Timing:** starter 5 min, learn 24 min, demonstration 13 min, check 13 min, exit ticket 5 min.

**Values:** air about 340 m/s at room temperature (the syllabus says 330–350 m/s); water about 1500 m/s; steel about 5000–6000 m/s. These are typical values; the syllabus only asks for the order solid > liquid > gas.

**Common mistakes:**

- "Sound is faster in air because air is lighter."
- "Sound travels in space."
- Confusing sound waves with electromagnetic waves (radio).

**Language support:**

- *vocal cords* = the parts in your throat that vibrate when you speak.
- *medium* = the material a wave travels through.

**Differentiation:**

- Support: sentence cards to order: "The cone vibrates → it pushes the air particles → … ".
- Stretch: why does the bell jar never become completely silent? (The bell touches the base; the vacuum is not perfect.)

**Safety:** the bell jar is under low pressure: use a safety screen.

</Step>
