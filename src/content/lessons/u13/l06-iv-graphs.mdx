<Step kind="learn" title="The three graphs" minutes={15}>

<Figure svg={ivGraphsSvg()} caption="Current–voltage graphs." width="38rem" />

**Resistor (at constant temperature):**

- A **straight line through the origin**: $I$ is **directly proportional** to $V$.
- So the resistance $\dfrac{V}{I}$ is **constant**. Reversing the p.d. reverses the current, with the same size.

**Filament lamp:**

- A curve that gets **less steep** as $V$ increases (an S shape, through the origin).
- As the current increases, the filament gets **hotter**. The ions in the metal vibrate more, so the electrons collide more: the **resistance increases**.

**Diode:**

- In one direction (**reverse**), there is almost **no current**: very high resistance.
- In the other direction (**forward**), there is no current at first, then the current rises very quickly above about 0.6 V: low resistance.
- A diode lets current flow in **one direction only**.

</Step>

<Step kind="teacher" title="Teacher notes">

**Extended only** (4.2.4 S4). Core students can use the simulation to practise $R = \dfrac{V}{I}$ (lesson 13.4).

**Timing:** starter 5 min, simulation 22 min, learn 15 min, check 13 min, exit ticket 5 min.

**About the simulation:** the lamp model is $I \propto V^{0.55}$ (rated 6 V, 0.5 A). The diode model is an exponential that rises above about 0.5–0.6 V; a real circuit would need a protective resistor in series with the diode.

**Common mistakes:**

- Drawing the lamp curve bending upwards (it bends towards the V axis: less steep).
- Saying the resistance of a lamp is $\dfrac{1}{\text{gradient}}$ (use $\dfrac{V}{I}$ at a point).
- Forgetting the negative quadrant.

**Language support:**

- *forward* / *reverse* bias: the two ways to connect a diode.
- *steep* = rising quickly.

**Differentiation:**

- Support: three empty axes to sketch on, with the names of the components.
- Stretch: an LED is a diode that gives out light. How would its I–V graph look?

</Step>
