<Step kind="learn" title="Length and thickness" minutes={12}>

For a wire of one metal, at a constant temperature:

- A **longer** wire has a **bigger** resistance. (The electrons collide with more ions on the way.)
- A **thicker** wire (a bigger **cross-sectional area**) has a **smaller** resistance. (There is more space for the electrons to flow.)

<Figure svg={wireResistanceSvg()} caption="How length and area change the resistance of a wire." width="30rem" />

**Extended: the relationships**

- Resistance is **directly proportional** to length: $R \propto L$. Double the length → double the resistance.
- Resistance is **inversely proportional** to cross-sectional area: $R \propto \dfrac{1}{A}$. Double the area → half the resistance.

**Careful with diameter:** the cross-section of a wire is a circle: $A = \pi r^2$. **Twice** the diameter gives **4 times** the area, so **a quarter** of the resistance.

</Step>

<Step kind="do" title="Investigate length" minutes={18}>

**Equipment:** a 1 m length of thin constantan (or nichrome) wire taped to a metre rule, two crocodile clips, a cell, an ammeter, a voltmeter, leads.

1. Connect one clip at 0 cm. Connect the other clip at 10 cm.
2. Measure $V$ and $I$. Calculate $R = \dfrac{V}{I}$.
3. Repeat at 20, 30, … 100 cm.
4. Plot a graph of $R$ (vertical axis) against length (horizontal axis).

**Questions:**

1. What shape is your graph? Does it go through the origin?
2. What does this tell you about $R$ and the length?
3. Predict the resistance of 1 m of a wire with twice the cross-sectional area.

**Safety:** the wire can get hot: keep the current small and switch off between readings.

</Step>

<Step kind="teacher" title="Teacher notes">

**Core and Extended:** the qualitative relationships (4.2.4 C3) are Core. The proportional relationships (S5) are Extended.

**Timing:** starter 5 min, learn 12 min, investigation 18 min, practice 17 min, exit ticket 8 min.

**Practical tips:** constantan wire (about 0.3 mm diameter) gives resistances of a few ohms per metre. Use a single cell. Contact resistance at the clips can give a small intercept.

**Common mistakes:**

- "A thicker wire has more resistance because there is more metal."
- Twice the diameter → half the resistance (it is a quarter).

**Language support:**

- *directly proportional*: when one doubles, the other doubles.
- *inversely proportional*: when one doubles, the other halves.

**Differentiation:**

- Support: give the results table with lengths already filled.
- Stretch: why are the cables for a kettle thicker than the cables for a lamp?

</Step>
